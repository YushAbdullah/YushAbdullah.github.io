Prologue

The Lamp by the River

I grew up in Jeli, a small town in Kelantan, raised by my mother on a rubber farm at the edge of the jungle, not far from a river. There was a season in those years when we had very little. Some nights there was no electricity, and we lived by the light of a small wick lamp, its flame throwing long shadows across the walls.

I do not tell this for sympathy. Many people have known harder seasons than mine, and many are living through them now. I tell it because of what that lamp taught me, a lesson I have carried into every classroom, every meeting room and every product team since.

A small lamp does not light the whole house. It lights the page in front of you. It is enough to read by, enough to take the next step by, enough to keep going until morning. In the dark, you do not need to see the whole road. You need to see the next few metres, and the courage to walk them.

This book is about that flame, and about what we build with it: teams that trust each other, products that customers believe in, and organisations that can bend without breaking. If you are reading this in your own hard season, at home or at work, I want you to know one thing before anything else. A lamp is small, but it is enough to read by. And reading by a small light is how every great journey begins.

Little strokes fell great oaks.

Benjamin Franklin

Introduction

Two Kinds of Growth

There are two ways a person grows. One makes you stronger. The other makes you softer. For most of my working life I chased the first, and I did not notice the second quietly arriving.

Strength is what you build when you keep showing up. It is the extra hour before a deadline, the difficult conversation you do not postpone, the job you take because you need it and not because you want it. In business, strength looks like execution: shipping on time, hitting the number, holding the line when the pressure rises. It is visible, and it is good. Every organisation needs it to survive.

Wisdom is quieter. It shows up as patience with a difficult stakeholder, as listening a little longer before you speak, as asking your team what they already know before you tell them what you think. Wisdom does not make a leader harder. It makes a leader gentler, and that gentleness is not weakness. It is strength that has learned where to point itself.

I am, by my own description, a late bloomer with a big curiosity. Nothing in my career was solved overnight. I started from zero, built my skills one by one, learned to work under pressure and slowly found the courage to attempt things I had never done before. Today I work on hiring and workforce products, where people, data and artificial intelligence meet. The path here was not a straight line, and I have come to believe that most good paths are not.

This is not a memoir. My own story appears only where it helps to illustrate a point. The real subject is you, and the team you lead or hope to lead: how to make decisions under uncertainty, how to earn trust that survives a bad quarter, how to build products people actually need and how to stay human while doing all of it.

I wrote this for anyone who feels they are behind, and for the new manager who was promoted for being good at the work and now has to be good at people. Every role is training for the next one, if you are paying attention.

The strong get stronger because they keep going. The wise get softer because they learn why they are going. I hope this book helps you, and the people you lead, do both.

Part One

Roots

Chapter 1

Discipline Before Dreams

Every organisation I have worked with had more ideas than it could use. What it rarely had enough of was discipline: the steady, unglamorous habit of doing the important thing, again and again, after the excitement had worn off.

I learned discipline early, not as a virtue I chose but as a necessity that chose me. When you grow up with little, you learn quickly that motivation comes and goes like the weather, but discipline stays. Later, when I was first given responsibility for other people at school, I discovered its second half. Authority means nothing if people do not respect you, and respect does not come pinned to your shirt with a badge. It is earned slowly, by being consistent, fair and present, day after ordinary day.

That is still the best description of leadership I know.

Teams do not follow the leader with the most inspiring speech. They follow the leader whose behaviour they can predict. If you keep your promises when nobody would notice you breaking them, your team learns that your word is safe to build on. If you change direction every time you feel a new burst of enthusiasm, they learn to wait you out. Consistency is the quiet foundation of trust.

The same is true of companies. The most innovative firms are rarely the ones with the most ideas. They are the ones disciplined enough to execute the few ideas that matter, to say no to the rest and to keep going through the long middle of a project, when the launch party is far away and the problems are close. Discipline turns a dream into a plan, and a plan into a result.

In practice, discipline at work looks small. A weekly review that actually happens. A backlog that is kept honest. A decision written down so that nobody has to guess what was agreed. A habit of learning something every week, even when the calendar is full. None of these will make headlines. Together, they decide whether a team delivers.

Discipline is the bridge between goals and accomplishment.

Jim Rohn

Lesson: Talent opens the door, but discipline keeps you in the room. Build reliable habits for yourself and your team before you need them, because trust is built on what people can predict.

Chapter 2

Two Lenses

My first field of study was security. People are sometimes surprised that someone who now works in product began there. I am not. Security taught me to look for risk before it arrives, and that habit has never left me.

Security is really the study of what can go wrong. You learn to walk into a room and ask, almost without thinking, where the exits are, who has access and what happens if one piece fails. Years later, writing risk registers and reviewing project plans, I found myself asking the same questions in a different language.

Later I studied public policy, and it opened a wider window. Security asks how to protect a system. Policy asks what the system is for, whom it serves and whom it leaves out. One lens looks for the crack in the wall. The other asks why the wall was built, and who is standing on each side of it.

Every business decision needs both lenses.

The first lens is risk. Before you launch a product, enter a market or restructure a team, you need someone who asks the uncomfortable questions. What happens if the vendor fails? What if the data is wrong? What if customers use this feature in a way we never intended? Leaders who skip this step are not bold. They are simply unprepared.

The second lens is impact. Every decision lands on real people: customers, employees, partners, communities. A pricing change that looks clever in a spreadsheet may quietly push out the customers who trusted you first. A new process that saves management an hour may cost the frontline team a day. Good leaders ask who is affected before they ask how much it will save.

I have also learned that your first field is never wasted, whatever it is. An engineer brings precision to strategy. A teacher brings clarity to product. A nurse brings calm to a crisis. When you hire, look for these hidden lenses. When you build a team, mix them deliberately. Diversity of background is not a slogan. It is a way of seeing more of the problem.

Lesson: Look at every decision twice: once for the risk, once for the people it affects. And treat every person's first field as a lens your team can borrow, not a gap to apologise for.

Part Two

Voice

Chapter 3

Finding Your Voice

The first time I truly stood up to speak, it was not for myself. It was to argue for a budget to help a community. I remember the dryness in my mouth, the paper trembling slightly in my hand, and the feeling that every face in the room was waiting for me to fail. I did not speak perfectly. But I spoke, and the community was heard. That was the day I learned that courage is not the absence of fear. It is having a reason bigger than the fear.

Every leader needs a voice. You will need it to pitch an idea to a sceptical board, to explain a hard decision to a worried team, to tell a customer the truth when the truth is inconvenient. Many capable people stay invisible at work, not because their ideas are weak but because they never learned to say them out loud.

A voice is not a gift you are born with. It is a muscle. You train it by using it in rooms where you are slightly out of your depth, and then going back to rooms that are slightly bigger. Over the years I spoke on conference panels and at forums, and each time the room was a little larger and my hands shook a little less. Strength comes from repetition.

Wisdom came later. The best speakers I met were not the loudest. They listened to the question fully before answering it, admitted when they did not know and made the audience feel capable rather than small. In business, the same is true of the best pitches. Investors and customers do not remember the cleverest slide. They remember the person who understood their problem.

If you lead a team, your job is also to help others find their voice. Notice who never speaks in meetings, and ask them directly, in a way that feels safe. Give junior colleagues the chance to present their own work. A team where only one person talks is a team using a fraction of its intelligence.

Seek first to understand, then to be understood.

Stephen R. Covey

Lesson: Speak often enough to become strong, and listen long enough to become worth hearing. Then make room for the quiet voices on your team, because the best idea in the room is not always the loudest.

Chapter 4

The Backstage

Most of the work that makes a conference succeed is invisible. Name tags laid out in neat rows. Seating plans checked and checked again. Timings, the microphone that works, the guest who arrives at the wrong entrance and needs someone calm to meet them. I learned all of this as part of a conference secretariat early in my career.

A secretariat is the team nobody thanks when things go right. If the programme runs on time, people credit the speakers. If it runs late, everyone looks for someone to blame. That taught me to measure my work by what did not go wrong.

Every business has a backstage. Operations, finance, support, compliance, data, the people who keep systems running at night. When they do their jobs well, nobody notices. Customers simply have a good experience, and the people on stage take the bow. Leaders who forget the backstage end up with brilliant strategies that collapse on contact with reality.

Behind-the-scenes work is also where I first practised what I now call product operations. You map every step, plan for the failure points and make sure the people on stage never have to think about logistics. In innovation, this is the unglamorous truth: every breakthrough depends on someone running the backstage well.

As a leader, you can do better than hope your backstage team rewards itself. Name their work in public. Invite them into planning early, because they know where the plan will break. Promote from the backstage as readily as from the stage. A culture that only celebrates visible work will slowly lose the people who make everything else possible.

Lesson: If you want to lead the stage one day, learn to run the backstage first. And as a leader, make the invisible work visible, because the people who prevent problems deserve as much credit as the people who present solutions.

Chapter 5

The World at One Table

At a Model United Nations conference I sat behind a country placard with a laptop, a notepad and a group of delegates I had met only that morning. Committee rooms like that teach diplomacy faster than any textbook. You have a position to defend, a clock running down and people across the table with positions of their own.

My first instinct was to win the debate. I prepared arguments, statistics and rebuttals, and I was proud of them. But resolutions do not pass because one delegate is the best speaker. They pass because enough delegates feel heard to sign. The real work happens in the corridors and in the small huddles around a laptop, where you quietly rewrite a clause so that someone else can say yes.

Business works the same way. Engineering, design, quality assurance, sales, finance and legal each arrive at a meeting with a position, the way delegates arrive with their placards. Each is right about something. A leader who tries to win every argument will find that decisions get made, and then quietly unmade, because the people who lost never truly agreed.

Stakeholder management is not about persuasion alone. It is about understanding what each party needs to protect. The finance lead needs predictability. The engineer needs time to do it properly. The sales team needs something to sell this quarter. When you know what each person is guarding, you can often find the clause that lets everyone say yes.

I still think about that committee table when I run a requirements workshop. My job is the same one I had then: listen to every position, find the shared ground, write it down clearly and move the resolution forward.

If you want to go fast, go alone. If you want to go far, go together.

Proverb

Lesson: The strongest argument rarely wins. The most inclusive one does. Before any big decision, ask what each stakeholder needs to protect, and design the agreement around it.

Part Three

Service

Chapter 6

After the Water Went Down

During my university years I helped to start a student volunteer team, and I stayed involved for several years. The idea was simple. People learn teamwork best when the stakes are real, so we would learn by serving.

The mission I remember most was helping families after a flood. We brought aid packs, and the packs mattered. But the part I am proudest of was the confidence work: sessions to help the community, especially the young, believe in themselves again after the water went down.

Disaster relief teaches you quickly that strength is not about you. You can carry boxes all day, but if you do not listen to what a family actually needs, you are only busy. The volunteers who made the biggest difference were the ones who sat down, asked questions and stayed patient.

This is the first lesson of customer focus, and of leadership. Being busy is not the same as being useful. Many companies launch features nobody asked for, and many managers fill their teams' calendars with activity that changes nothing. The cure is the same in a flood zone and in a boardroom: go to where the people are, ask what they need and listen to the answer, even when it is not the answer you prepared for.

Much of my sense of service came from one of my lecturers. She gave enormous time to volunteerism without ever asking what she would get in return. Instead, she asked us what value we needed to become catalysts for our community. That question changed how I thought about leadership. It is not "What can I get?" but "What can I help others become?"

The volunteer team also taught me how to build something that outlives a single event. A volunteer platform needs structure: roles, briefings, safety plans, follow-ups and new leaders coming through behind you. Without knowing the words for it, I was learning programme management, the same discipline that carries products from idea to launch. Above all, I learned that a leader's real test is whether the work continues when they step away. If everything depends on you, you have built a dependency, not a team.

Many hands make light work.

English proverb

Lesson: Service builds strength in the body; listening builds wisdom in the heart. Lead like a servant: find out what people truly need, and build a team strong enough to carry on without you.

Chapter 7

Teaching Is Learning Twice

The first time you teach something, you discover how little you understood it. That happened to me in my first study skills class. Standing at the front of a room, I watched students present to each other and realised my job was not to know everything. It was to help them find the way on their own.

Over the years I taught in different settings, including classrooms of young children learning Mathematics and English. Those rooms were loud, untidy and entirely worth it. They taught me more about leadership than many management courses.

Teaching made me softer in the best way. A child who is struggling does not need a stronger teacher. They need a more patient one. You lower your voice, slow down and find another way to explain the same thing, and then another, until the light comes on in their eyes. That patience is a skill, and it took me years to learn.

Good managers are teachers, whether or not they think of themselves that way. Every time you onboard a new hire, explain a strategy or give feedback, you are teaching. The manager who says "just figure it out" and the manager who takes ten minutes to explain the reasoning behind a decision will build very different teams. One builds people who wait for instructions. The other builds people who can make the next decision themselves.

Teaching also made me a better product person. Writing a clear user story is very like planning a lesson. You begin with what the learner, or the user, already knows and build one step at a time. Innovation that people cannot understand is innovation they will not use. The same applies inside the company: a change programme that staff do not understand is a change programme they will quietly resist.

As the twig is bent, so grows the tree.

English proverb

Lesson: If you want to master something, teach it. Treat every briefing, onboarding and feedback conversation as a lesson, and build a team that understands the why, not only the what.

Part Four

Grit

Chapter 8

The Unglamorous Years

Not every chapter of a career looks good on a stage. For several years I took jobs that paid the bills and taught me more than I ever expected them to. There was also a season when the path ahead was unclear and I could not see where it led. I do not hide those years. They are where I was tested.

One of those jobs was on a customer service helpdesk, during the long months of the pandemic. The work was repetitive and often stressful. The queue never truly emptied. Every message was from someone worried about a payment, a dispute or a service that had gone wrong. Angry customers do not care about your qualifications.

That job taught me empathy at scale. Behind every ticket was a person worried about their money. I learned to answer the worry first and the question second. It is the most useful product lesson I have ever learned, and I learned it in an inbox.

Every leader should spend time in the inbox. The frontline is where your strategy meets reality. It is where you learn which features confuse people, which policies feel unfair and which promises your marketing made that your product cannot keep. Leaders who never sit with customer complaints end up making decisions about customers they have never heard.

I used those years to reskill, upskill and multiskill. I went to every training, workshop and conference I could, not because anyone required it but because I knew the job I had was not the job I wanted. That habit of learning on purpose, even in a role you plan to leave, is one of the best investments any professional can make.

These were the years that made me strong. No audience, no applause, only work done well, every day. If you are in your unglamorous years now, keep going. Nobody is watching, which means you are free to become very good. And if you lead people who are in theirs, notice them. A word of recognition in a hard season can carry someone a very long way.

Where there is a will, there is a way.

English proverb

Lesson: The quiet years are not a detour; they are where the foundation is poured. Stay close to the frontline, because the customer's worry is your best product brief. Risking is better than regretting.

Chapter 9

The Beginner's Mind

Later in my career I went back to university to study business. I was older than many of my classmates, and I carried years of real work into every case study. That turned out to be an advantage. Theories about customer relationships, digital transformation and market strategy were not abstract to me. I had lived them, one customer email at a time.

Study taught me that innovation is never only about technology. It is about reading the market, understanding the customer and moving before the window closes. Companies rarely fail because they lack new tools. They fail because they adopt tools without changing the way people work, or because they misread what their customers actually value. Digitalisation is as much a people project as a technical one.

But the deeper lesson of returning to study was about attitude. Earlier in my career I wanted to prove I was clever. Back in the classroom, I was content to be the one asking questions. That shift, from proving to learning, was the moment I began to grow wiser.

Leaders need this shift more than anyone. The more senior you become, the more people expect you to have answers, and the more tempting it is to pretend. But markets change, technologies change and the lessons of the last decade may be the mistakes of the next. Artificial intelligence alone is reshaping how we hire, work and decide. A leader who cannot learn, unlearn and relearn will soon be leading yesterday's organisation.

Build that habit into your culture. Make it safe to say "I don't know yet". Reward people who ask good questions, not only those who give quick answers. Set aside time for learning, and protect it, especially when the calendar is full.

Yesterday I was clever, so I wanted to change the world. Today I am wise, so I am changing myself.

attributed to Rumi

Lesson: It is never too late to be a student. Keep a beginner's mind, and build a culture where learning, unlearning and relearning are part of the work, not a break from it.

Part Five

Leadership

Chapter 10

Building Products People Trust

Today I work on hiring and workforce products: the systems that help organisations find people, bring them on board and support them through their working lives. It is work where people, data and artificial intelligence meet, and it has taught me that the hardest part of product work is rarely the technology. It is the people, the timeline and the trust.

A dashboard is useless if leaders do not believe its numbers. A feature fails if the team that must use it was never asked what it needs. An AI tool that makes decisions nobody can explain will be resisted, and rightly so. Trust is the real product. Everything else is how you deliver it.

Some of the most valuable work I have done looked very small from the outside: a simpler approval process, a clearer report, a manual task taken away from a team that had been doing it for years. None of it was glamorous. But each change gave people back time, and each one earned a little more trust for the next change. Innovation inside an organisation is built this way, one credible improvement at a time.

This is where every earlier lesson pays its dividend. The security lens finds the risk. The policy lens asks who is affected. The backstage habit makes sure the rollout works. The committee table drafts the requirement everyone can sign. The helpdesk hears the worry behind the request. The teacher explains it one step at a time. And the small lamp reminds you that every user is a person with a story.

Certifications and frameworks help, and I have collected my share. Yet the most important skill on any product team appears on no certificate. It is the willingness to listen before you build.

Lesson: Strong teams ship fast. Wise teams ship what people actually need, in a way they can trust. Measure success not only by what you launch, but by whether people believe in it and use it.

Chapter 11

The Seat and the Sense

There are moments in every career when we find ourselves outside the circle: not invited into the room, not mentioned in the thread, not included on the committee. The door closes, the meeting goes on without you, and it is easy to feel unseen. But leadership, I have learned, is not about the seat we hold. It is about the sense we bring.

True leadership begins with awareness: of people, of purpose and of the quiet details others overlook. It is the ability to sense when a teammate is struggling before a word is spoken, or when an idea needs protecting before it is dismissed. Leadership is not authority. It is empathy in motion.

Leadership is also how we respond to exclusion. We can dwell in disappointment, or we can turn that energy into initiative. When we act from clarity rather than ego, our actions speak louder than any title. People begin to see leadership in us not because we claim it, but because they feel it.

Self-awareness is part of the sense. I know my own strengths and my own edges. I am detail-oriented by nature, and I always see sequences and consequences. That is a strength, but it can also keep my eyes fixed on the next step rather than the horizon. Part of my growth as a leader is learning to lift my gaze to the bigger picture, and to trust others with the details I would once have held tightly in my own hands. Every leader has a strength that, overused, becomes a weakness. Knowing yours is the beginning of leading well.

So if you are reading this and feel left out of the table, remember this. Leadership is not about the seat you sit in. It is about the sense you bring into the room, even when the room does not yet include you.

Lesson: A title can be given; leadership can only be earned, and it is earned in how you treat people. Look for the people leading without a title in your organisation, and give them room to grow.

Chapter 12

Beyond Micromanagement

Leadership, at its core, is about navigating complexity: balancing roles, responsibilities and relationships. Yet many leaders fall into the trap of micromanagement, driven by a misunderstanding of accountability. It stifles productivity and leads to burnout, for the team and for the leader alike.

In a case study of micromanagement, I set out three principles for sustainable leadership:

  1. Do the hardest things first. Tackle the difficult challenge proactively, before it grows.
  2. Work-life balance. Cultivate harmony between personal and professional life, for yourself and for your people.
  3. Embrace the grey. Recognise the value of ambiguity for long-term growth.

Consider two everyday scenes. You urge your child to wake early for school, and the child protests from under the blanket, "But it's Monday." Or you order a latte and ask for no syrup, only to find it overly sweet. In both cases the problem is not only the outcome. It is communication and decision-making.

As leaders, communication and adaptability are everything. Saying "no" outright may solve a problem for a day, but it is not a strategy. Leaders should aim for structured decision-making, with a holistic approach that aligns with the wider business strategy.

The danger lies in the grey areas, the spaces where solutions exist but remain unexplored because of indecision or rigidity. Leaders who hesitate too long miss the very opportunities where growth and innovation live.

A well-known bestseller on attitude reminds us that leadership is not only a matter of skill but of mindset. A structured leader focuses on adaptability, clarity and continuous improvement. As one scholar observed, "We don't have business problems; we have personal problems that reflect in our business." Unresolved personal challenges quietly become professional inefficiencies.

Leadership is not about perfection. It is about navigating imperfection with purpose and grace.

Lesson: Trust is the opposite of micromanagement. Set direction, agree what good looks like, then let your people walk.

Chapter 13

Empathy, Friendship and Appreciation

When someone comes into your life, appreciate them. Do not ignore them once you have what you wanted.

Relationships at work are harder than they look. We spend years searching for the best colleagues, partners and friends, and if we demand perfection from them, we should first look into ourselves, for none of us is truly perfect.

Some people draw a border around their professional relationships. I will stay close to him because I can use his ability. I will stay close to her because she can help my career. I will stay until he is useless to me. Imagine playing a part with someone's feelings and calling it teamwork. Once that trust breaks, it never returns with the same energy. Something in the room changes, and everyone feels it.

What does empathy mean to me? It means to feel with someone: to listen, to withhold judgement and to ask questions. Empathy sounds like "How did that make you feel?" or "Do you want to talk about it?" It takes time and it takes consideration. But it creates comfort and connection, and connection is the foundation of every team, every customer relationship and every product people love.

Appreciation is empathy put into action. Thank people specifically, for what they did and why it mattered. Remember the colleague who helped you when you were new, and help the next new person in the same way. Stay in touch with people after the project ends. Business is a long game, and the people you treat well on the way up are the ones who will trust you later.

I learned the deepest empathy at home, from my mother, who worked hard for so long and never once made me feel I was a burden. Whatever softness I bring to a team, I learned first from her.

No act of kindness, no matter how small, is ever wasted.

Aesop

Lesson: Appreciate people while they are with you, and say so specifically. Empathy is not a soft skill. It is the hardest skill, and the most valuable one a leader can bring to a team or a customer.

Chapter 14

The Wise Get Softer

There is a quote I return to whenever I feel slow:

No matter how many mistakes you make or how slow you progress, you are still way ahead of everyone who isn't trying.

Tony Robbins

That sentence holds the first half of a truth I have lived. The strong get stronger because they keep trying. Every hard season, every difficult room, every inbox and every sprint adds a little more strength. None of it is wasted, not even the parts that feel like detours at the time.

The second half, that the wise get softer, took me much longer to understand. When I was younger I thought wisdom would make me sharper, quicker to answer and harder to argue with. It did the opposite. The wiser I became, the more I paused. I asked more questions. I said "I don't know" more easily. I became gentler with colleagues, with customers and, slowest of all, with myself.

Organisations go through the same journey. Young companies survive on strength: speed, hustle, long nights. The ones that last learn softness too: listening to customers, caring for their people, admitting mistakes quickly and adapting before they are forced to. A company that only knows how to push will eventually break something it cannot fix. A company that learns to bend will outlast the storm.

Right now I am still a beginner in many things. I am learning perseverance, resilience and calm. I am learning to let go of the need to control every detail and to see the bigger picture. I am learning that improving by one per cent a day, quietly and without applause, is the surest way to climb.

Softness is not the absence of strength. Lao Tzu wrote that nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water, yet nothing can surpass it in overcoming the hard and the strong. A river is soft, and it carves valleys. I grew up near one. I should have known.

The bamboo that bends is stronger than the oak that resists.

Proverb

So here is what I believe now. Build your strength through effort, because no one can do the work for you. Then let wisdom soften how you use it. Speak, but listen first. Lead, but serve first. Build products, but understand people first. And when you are afraid, remember that risking is better than regretting.

The strong get stronger. The wise get softer. The best leaders, in time, become both.

Lesson: Build strength through effort, then let wisdom soften how you use it. Teams and companies that can both push and bend are the ones that last.

Afterword

Your Turn

I hope these pages have given you permission to see your own path as one connected journey, the detours included. Take a quiet hour, find a corner with good light, and use these questions to reflect on how you work and lead.

  1. What was your first field, and how does it still shape the way you see problems?
  2. When did you last speak in a room where you felt out of your depth? What did it teach you?
  3. What backstage work happens in your team that nobody thanks? How will you make it visible?
  4. Think of a time you won an argument but lost the agreement. What would the softer approach have been?
  5. When did you last listen directly to a customer or a frontline colleague? What did you hear?
  6. What are you learning right now as a beginner?
  7. Where in your leadership are you strong, but not yet soft?

Keep trying. Keep listening. The strong get stronger, and the wise get softer.
